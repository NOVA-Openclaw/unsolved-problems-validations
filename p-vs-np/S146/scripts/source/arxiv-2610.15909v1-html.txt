Abstract.

The extension complexity xc(P)\xc(P) of a polytope PP is the minimum number of facets of a polytope that projects onto it. Motivated by the complexity of neural networks, Hertrich and Loho introduced the virtual extension complexity vxc(P)\vxc(P) of a polytope PP, which is the minimum of xc(Q)+xc(R)\xc(Q)+\xc(R) among all polytopes QQ and RR such that P+Q=RP+Q=R. We show the following results pertaining to virtual extension complexity.

First, we resolve the core open problem on the topic by showing the virtual extension complexity can be exponentially smaller than extension complexity. Second, we show there are matroid base polytopes with exponential virtual extension complexity by adapting a semi-algebraic counting argument by Hrubeš that extends the counting arguments of Rothvoß.
Finally, we show that the maximum virtual extension complexity of a polygon with nn vertices is Θ(n)\Theta(\sqrt{n}). This resolves an analogue of a question on extension complexity of Fiorini, Rothvoß, and Tiwary in 2011 in the case of virtual extension complexity.
Introduction

Feasible sets of systems of linear inequalities, also known as polyhedra, are a fundamental modeling tool for optimization problems.
The running time of algorithms for solving these problems grows with the number of inequalities.
An approach to overcome this obstacle is to use an extended formulation, a description in a higher-dimensional space, and then project to the original components.
This can significantly reduce the required number of inequalities.
The minimal number of inequalities in such a description is known as extension complexity.
Extended formulations are among our strongest tools for solving combinatorial optimization problems, and as a consequence extension complexity has been studied extensively [5, 7].

Hertrich and Loho recently proposed a generalization of extension complexity motivated by neural networks [11]. See Subsection 1.1.3 for an overview of the connection. The virtual extension complexity vxc(P)\vxc(P) of a polytope PP is the minimum of the sum xc(Q)+xc(R)\xc(Q)+\xc(R) over all polytopes Q,RQ,R such that P+Q=RP+Q=R, where P+Q={x+y:x∈P,y∈Q}P+Q=\{x+y:x\in P,y\in Q\} is the Minkowski sum.
This allows for even more flexibility in the representation of the original optimization problem over PP: you solve it over QQ and RR, and then take the difference of optimal values to get the optimal value over PP. However, there is a trade-off. Minkowski sums generally yield much more complicated polytopes. In fact, at the conclusion of [11], they note that there is no known example where extension complexity and virtual extension complexity differ. A priori, they could always be equal. Question 5.1 in [11] asks whether this is the case.

Our first main result establishes that xc\xc and vxc\vxc of a polytope can be substantially different.

Theorem 1.1.

Let n≥4n\geq 4 be even, and let Pn⊆ℝ([n]2)P_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}} be the 0/10/1-polytope given by the convex hull of indicator vectors of edge sets of the complete graph such that the corresponding spanning subgraph contains a connected component with an odd number of vertices. Then xc(Pn)≥2Ω(n)\xc(P_{n})\geq 2^{\Omega(n)} and vxc(Pn)=O(n8)\vxc(P_{n})=O(n^{8}).

To prove this, we draw on classical results in combinatorial optimization. In breakthrough work, Rothvoß [20] introduced a powerful technique to show that the matching polytope has exponential extension complexity.
The family of polytopes PnP_{n} is sufficiently similar to the matching polytope for Rothvoß’ technique to still apply to give exponential lower bounds on their extension complexity. In contrast, we prove that it has polynomial virtual extension complexity by using the connection to neural networks for representing min-cut problems from [13].

This gives hope that virtual extension complexity could be a better model for polynomial time solvability. Could it be that most popular polynomial time solvable problems admit polynomial sized virtual extended formulations? We study this question next.

To do this, we consider 0/10/1-polytopes, that is, those polytopes whose vertices have only components 00 or 11.
Restricting edge directions to differences of standard basis vectors in ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} yields matroid base polytopes, a class of particular importance in combinatorial optimization.
Upper bounds are known for regular matroids, which can be defined as those matroids that can be represented over any field.
More precisely, by [1], their extension complexity is upper bounded by O(n6)O(n^{6}), while their virtual extension complexity has been shown in [12] to be upper bounded by O(n3)O(n^{3}).
Concerning lower bounds, using a counting-based argument, Rothvoß [21] showed that most 0/1 polytopes, and even almost all matroid base polytopes, have exponential extension complexity.

Using the idea of counting those subsets of {0,1}n\{0,1\}^{n} expressible by real-algebraic formulas, Hrubeš [14] derived a slightly weaker bound though with a more flexible framework.
In Section 3, we show how this technique can be applied to
obtain lower bounds on the virtual extension complexity of almost all matroid base polytopes.
The following is a direct consequence of Theorem 3.1 using that there are doubly-exponentially many matroid base polytopes [6].

Theorem 1.2.

There exists ε′>0\varepsilon^{\prime}>0 such that asymptotically almost every matroid MM on the ground set [n][n] satisfies vxc(B(M))≥2ε′n\vxc(B(M))\geq 2^{\varepsilon^{\prime}n}, where B(M)B(M) is the base polytope of MM.

Thus, even classical problems that are solvable in polynomial time may still have no polynomial sized virtual extended formulation.

Finally, we focus on the case of polygons, which has received substantial attention in the extension complexity literature. The core question, asked originally in 2011 in [8], is what the worst-case extension complexity of a polygon with nn vertices is, and they prove a lower bound of Ω(n)\Omega(\sqrt{n}) and no nontrivial upper bound. The first upper bounds less than nn of roughly ⌈6n/7⌉\lceil 6n/7\rceil were attained independently by Shitov [22] and Padrol and Pfeifle [18]. Shitov then improved this upper bound to a first sublinear bound of o(n)o(n) in a preprint and then later strengthened this to an upper bound of O(n2/3)O(n^{2/3}) in [23]. In the case of random polygons, combined results of Padrol [19] and Kwan, Sauermann, and Zhao [15] imply that the expected extension complexity of a random polygon with nn vertices is Θ(n)\Theta(\sqrt{n}). Furthermore, for cyclic polygons, i.e., polygons inscribed in a circle, Kwan, Sauermann, and Zhao showed in the same work that their maximum extension complexity is O(n)O(\sqrt{n}) and conjectured that this should be the case for all polygons.

Motivated by their work, we asked the same question for virtual extension complexity, and managed a complete resolution via a geometric argument extending the ideas from [15]:

Theorem 1.3.

The maximum virtual extension complexity of a polygon with nn vertices is Θ(n)\Theta(\sqrt{n}).

It remains an open question whether in the case of polygons, virtual extension complexity can be significantly smaller than extension complexity. If not, then our result implies that the extension complexity of a polygon will be at most O(n)O(\sqrt{n}). We propose this as a novel approach to that conjecture.

A further significant question left open by our work is to determine the worst-case asymptotic virtual extension complexity of the matching polytopes. Furthermore, considering the original motivation of vxc\vxc via neural networks, it remains an open question whether the support function of the matching polytope can be represented by polynomial size neural networks. This stronger question is even open for the support function of the Birkhoff polytope, corresponding to maximum-weight bipartite perfect matching.

1.1. Preliminaries

1.1.1. Extended formulations

Let P⊆ℝnP\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} be a polyhedron.
We say that a polyhedron QQ in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} is an extension of PP, if there is a projection π:ℝd→ℝn\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n} such that π(Q)=P\pi(Q)=P.
More formally, we have A∈ℝm×dA\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}, b∈ℝmb\in\mathbb{R}^{m}, C∈ℝn×dC\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d} and c∈ℝnc\in\mathbb{R}^{n} such that

Q={y∈ℝd:Ay≤b}andP={Cy+c:y∈Q}.Q=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:Ay\leq b\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P=\{Cy+c:y\in Q\}.

Then, (A,b,C,c)(A,b,C,c) is called an extended formulation of PP.
Note that if PP is a polytope, then we can assume without loss of generality that d≤md\leq m.
The extension complexity xc(P)\xc(P) is defined as the smallest number of inequalities (number of rows of AA) of any extended formulation of PP (note that we do not use equations in the definition of extended formulations, since they can be projected out).

1.1.2. Virtual extended formulations

Recall the Minkowski sum of two subsets XX and YY of ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} denoted by X+Y:={x+y:x∈X,y∈Y}X+Y:=\{x+y:x\in X,y\in Y\}.
While we just defined extension complexity more generally for (potentially unbounded) polyhedra, virtual extension complexity is only defined for polytopes. See the conclusion of Section 4 of [11] for discussion as to why.
The virtual extension complexity of a polytope P⊆ℝnP\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} is

vxc(P):=min{xc(Q)+xc(R):Q,R polytopes with P+Q=R;}.\vxc(P):=\min\left\{\xc(Q)+\xc(R):Q,R\text{ polytopes with }P+Q=R;\right\}.

Linear optimization over a polytope P⊆ℝnP\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} can equivalently be phrased as evaluating the support function, that is, the function hP(c):=max{c⊤x:x∈P}h_{P}(c):=\max\{c^{\top}x:x\in P\} for a fixed c∈ℝnc\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For polytopes P,Q,R⊆ℝnP,Q,R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} with P+Q=RP+Q=R, we have for all c∈ℝnc\in\mathbb{R}^{n} that hP(c)+hQ(c)=hR(c)h_{P}(c)+h_{Q}(c)=h_{R}(c).
This shows that indeed linear optimization over PP can be reduced to linear optimization over extended formulations of QQ and RR; see also [11] for details on how to recover the actual optimal solution.
Hence, virtual extension complexity is actually a reasonable complexity measure for linear optimization over PP.

1.1.3. Neural network complexity

For a general introduction to neural networks, we refer to [10]. We use the same definitions and conventions as in [11].
In particular, when talking about neural networks, we generally refer to rank-2 maxout networks as defined in [11]. Up to a small constant factor in size, every such network is equivalent to the well-known model of ReLU feedforward networks.

A maxout neural network is based on a directed, acyclic graph with node set VV and arcs A⊆V×VA\subseteq V\times V, where the nodes are called neurons and act as computational units.
The d≥1d\geq 1 neurons with in-degree zero are called input neurons. All other s≥1s\geq 1 neurons are maxout units. Every maxout unit computes the maximum of two linear combinations of the outputs of its predecessors.
We assume that among the maxout units there exists exactly one neuron with out-degree zero, the output neuron.
The size of the neural network is defined as the number ss of maxout units.

Note that we define neural networks without biases. For our purposes, this is without loss of generality, compare [11, Remark 2.1].
The functions represented by such maxout neural networks without biases are exactly the positively homogeneous CPWL (continuous piecewise linear) functions [2]. Note, however, that the required number of neurons can be large.
On the other hand, positively homogeneous CPWL functions are exactly the differences of support functions of polytopes.
For a polytope PP, we define the neural network complexity nnc(P)\nnc(P) as the minimum number of neurons to represent its support function hPh_{P} by a maxout neural network.

Hertrich and Loho introduced this notion and showed in [11, Theorem 4.1] the following inequality.

Proposition 1.4.

For any polytope PP, vxc(P)≤4⋅nnc(P)\vxc(P)\leq 4\cdot\nnc(P).

As mentioned above, while we introduced neural network complexity using maxout networks, this carries over to ReLU-networks with the cost of a small constant factor [11, Remark 2.2].

We use Proposition 1.4 to prove the upper bound on virtual extension complexity in Section 2, where we construct a neural network to represent the support function of the considered polytope PnP_{n}. Furthermore, combining Proposition 1.4 with our counting-based lower bound for matroid polytopes (Theorem 1.2), we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.5.

There exists ε′′>0\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}>0 such that asymptotically almost every matroid MM on the ground set [n][n] satisfies nnc(B(M))>2ε′′n\nnc(B(M))>2^{\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}n}, where B(M)B(M) is the base polytope of MM.

Hence, there is a family of matroids for which every ReLU network exactly computing the maximum-weight basis value has size 2Ω(n)2^{\Omega(n)}.
Exponential Separation

We organize the proof of Theorem 1.1 into two parts.
The proof of the lower bound on the ordinary extension complexity follows from the work of Walter and Weltge [24] on the odd-cut dominant, building on Rothvoß’s exponential lower bound for the perfect matching polytope [20].
For the upper bound on the virtual extension complexity, we express the support function of the polytope through certain minimum cut values.
Then, we build a polynomial size neural network which exactly computes this expression.
This yields an explicit virtual extended formulation of size O(n8)O(n^{8}) by Proposition 1.4.

Dn=conv{χδ(S):S⊆V,|S| odd}+ℝ+E.D_{n}=\mathrm{conv}\{\chi^{\delta(S)}:S\subseteq V,\ |S|\text{ odd}\}+\mathbb{R}_{+}^{E}.
